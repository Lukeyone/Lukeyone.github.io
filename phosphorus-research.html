Peak Phosphorus, Food Security and Geopolitical Conflict

A systems analysis of whether phosphorus scarcity is best understood as geological exhaustion, declining accessibility, industrial dependency or geopolitical concentration—and how those mechanisms could affect global food security.

Request the full reportBack to research

Research question

Phosphorus is indispensable to agriculture and has no direct substitute in plant nutrition. Yet “peak phosphorus” is often reduced to a single depletion date. This research separates several different questions:

  • How large are physical phosphate resources?
  • How much material is economically classified as reserves under current technology and prices?
  • Can fertiliser be produced and delivered affordably when ore quality, sulphur, ammonia, water, energy, transport and processing capacity are constrained?
  • How do export controls, territorial disputes and concentrated supply alter food-system risk?
Core conclusion: an accessibility and systems crisis is more credible than a precise global “run-out” date. The risk lies in cost, quality, processing dependencies, market power and geopolitical disruption.

Method

Forecast lineage

Traced the frequently repeated 2070 peak-phosphorus forecast to its original modelling assumptions and compared it with subsequent criticism, reserve revisions and alternative interpretations.

Cross-domain synthesis

Integrated phosphate geology, beneficiation and processing, agronomy, fertiliser manufacture, reserve economics, trade concentration, logistics and geopolitical strategy.

Expert input

Interviewed PhD researchers internationally to identify contested assumptions, evidence gaps and areas where popular narratives oversimplify the science or supply chain.

Scenario architecture

Built causal pathways rather than a point forecast: ore-quality decline, processing-input stress, export restrictions, alliance formation, conflict exposure and food-price transmission.

Key findings

  • Resources are not reserves. Reserve estimates change with price, technology, knowledge and reporting rules; upward revisions do not eliminate access risk.
  • Ore tonnes are not fertiliser supply. Production depends on processing inputs, infrastructure and the ability to manage contaminants and waste.
  • Concentration matters. Large resource bases and export capacity are unevenly distributed, making policy and territorial conditions economically important.
  • Timing is uncertain. A fixed global peak date is less defensible than monitoring multiple stress indicators.
  • Recycling helps but is not frictionless. Recovery from wastewater, manure and waste streams requires infrastructure, energy, regulation and viable economics.
  • Conflict is one pathway, not a forecast. A “phosphorus cold war” scenario is useful for testing vulnerabilities, but the report does not claim that war is inevitable.

Why it matters for AI

Mineral-resource decisions require combining heterogeneous evidence with different scales, uncertainty and provenance. This research motivates future work in scientific machine learning and decision support:

  • multimodal mineral-prospectivity modelling;
  • spatially valid train/test design;
  • uncertainty-aware resource and supply-chain scenarios;
  • retrieval systems that distinguish geological, economic and policy claims;
  • human-reviewed tools for high-cost exploration and infrastructure decisions.

The project demonstrates domain learning, evidence synthesis and the ability to communicate uncertainty without flattening it into a misleading headline.

Limitations

  • This is an independent research synthesis, not a new global reserve estimate.
  • Reserve and trade data change over time and require periodic refresh.
  • Expert interviews informed interpretation but do not replace systematic primary-data collection.
  • The scenario pathways are analytical tools, not probability-weighted forecasts.
  • Local agronomic conditions and regional recycling potential vary substantially.
